Floating-Point Precision Pitfalls

Dhahran, 25 February 1991. A Patriot battery counts time in tenth-of-a-second ticks and multiplies by a 24-bit copy of $0.1$. But $0.1$ has no exact binary form, and chopped to 24 bits it becomes $209715/2097152 = 0.0999999046\ldots$, so every tick is short by about $9.5\times10^{-8}$ s. The error is never reset while the battery stays powered, so it grows in proportion to uptime. The radar places its range gate, the window where it expects the tracked target next, using that clock; multiply the time error by the Scud's closing speed of about 1676 m/s and the gate is displaced. After roughly 100 hours the clock is off by 0.34 s and the gate is half a kilometre off target: the incoming Scud falls outside it, the track is dropped, and no interceptor fires. The scene shows the gate walking off the target as uptime grows; the diagnostic plots the displacement against uptime and the gate's catch radius. 28 people died from one truncated constant.

WHAT TO TRY

  • Just watch: the system clock ages on its own and the interceptor goes from clean kills, through near misses, to a lost track. The intercept-confidence bar slides down continuously, there is no single instant it "switches off".
  • Grab the uptime slider to hold a moment: a thousandth-of-a-radian rounding of 0.1 per tick accumulates into the miss distance shown, growing in exact proportion to how long the battery has been powered.
  • Park it near 100 hours, the real Dhahran value: the gate is about half a kilometre off, far outside its radius, and the launcher never fires.
  • Tick patched software: the drift vanishes, every intercept is clean at any uptime. One corrected constant fixes it.
