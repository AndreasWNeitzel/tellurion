Quadtree N-body and the Barnes-Hut Approximation

A galaxy of N stars pulls on itself by gravity. Doing it honestly costs $N^2$ force pairs every step, which is hopeless for a real galaxy. Barnes-Hut cheats brilliantly: it bins the stars into an adaptive quadtree and, for any clump that is far enough away, replaces the whole clump with a single point at its center of mass. A cell of width $s$ seen from distance $d$ is lumped when $s/d \lt \theta$. The result is $O(N\log N)$, the algorithm that makes galaxy and cosmology simulations possible. The scene shows the disk orbiting a heavy core with the live quadtree drawn over it; the diagnostic plots the force-pair count against N, Barnes-Hut against the brute-force $N^2$.

WHAT TO TRY

  • Watch the quadtree subdivide finely where stars crowd together and stay coarse in the empty gaps; it follows the matter every step.
  • Switch to direct N²: the force-pair count jumps to the brute-force curve, far above Barnes-Hut, especially at large N.
  • Raise the opening angle θ: the tree lumps clumps more aggressively, fewer evaluations and a coarser (faster, less accurate) force.
  • Push N up and read the gap on the lower plot: Barnes-Hut grows like N log N while direct grows like N².
