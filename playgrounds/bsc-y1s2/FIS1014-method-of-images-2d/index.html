How a metal plane mirrors a charge

Hold a charge above a grounded sheet of metal and something has to give: a conductor cannot have any field along its surface, so the charges inside it rearrange until the field meets the metal at a perfect right angle everywhere. Working out that rearrangement looks hard, until you notice a trick. The field above the plane is exactly the field you would get by throwing away the metal and putting an equal and opposite charge at the mirror position below. That fictitious image charge reproduces the real induced surface charge perfectly, and it pulls the real charge toward the plane with a genuine, measurable force. The scene shows the charge above the conductor, its field lines bending in to strike the surface head-on, and the negative charge it induces pooling underneath; flip on the image and watch the conductor vanish into a clean dipole. The diagnostic plots the induced surface charge along the plane, a sharp bell whose total is exactly minus the real charge.

WHAT TO TRY

  • Drag the charge around above the plane: the field lines always meet the metal at a right angle, never at a slant.
  • Lower the charge toward the surface: the induced charge sharpens into a tighter, taller spike, and the attraction grows.
  • Switch to reveal image: the conductor disappears and a clean dipole appears, identical above the plane.
  • Read the lower plot: however you move the charge, the induced charge always integrates to exactly minus the real charge.
