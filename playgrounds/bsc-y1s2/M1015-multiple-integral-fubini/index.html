Multiple Integral Fubini

A double integral is the signed volume trapped between a surface and a patch of the plane. You can never add up a whole volume at once, so you slice it: cut the patch into thin vertical strips, find the area under the surface along each strip, then add the strips up; or cut it into horizontal strips and add those. These are the two iterated integrals, integrate over y first then x, or over x first then y. Fubini's theorem says that for a well-behaved surface it does not matter which way you slice: both orders give exactly the same number. That sounds obvious, but it is the permission slip behind half of multivariable calculus, letting you swap the order whenever one is easier. The scene shows the integrand as a colour map with a slab sweeping across a region you can resize; the diagnostic accumulates the volume for both slicing orders at once, two different running totals that land on precisely the same final value.

WHAT TO TRY

  • Watch the slab sweep across and the running total climb; the two orders take different routes but land on the same number.
  • Switch the slice order and see the scene sweep the other way, yet the final total is unchanged.
  • Drag the region corner to resize it; both running totals re-converge to the new exact value.
  • Pull the top edge down to y = pi/2: you capture only the positive hump and the total is maximal; extend to y = pi and the negative half cancels it back toward zero.
