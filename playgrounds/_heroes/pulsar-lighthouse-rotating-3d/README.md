# Pulsar Lighthouse (Hero)

A neutron star spins; the magnetic-dipole axis is off the spin axis;
each magnetic pole sweeps a narrow radio cone across the sky. When
the cone crosses your line of sight, you see a pulse. Pulsars are
seen because of geometry, not because they switch on and off.

## What to look for

The white glowing sphere is the neutron star. The vertical white
line is the spin axis $\Omega$. The cyan line is the magnetic
dipole, tilted by the obliquity $\alpha = 50^\circ$ and rotating with
the star. The two orange cones are the emission beams of half-angle
$\rho = 12^\circ$. The yellow dashed line is your line of sight at
inclination $i = 65^\circ$ from the spin axis, ending at a blue Earth
icon. The dashed cyan ellipses are the polar circles that the
magnetic poles trace out. On the right, the live pulse profile
$I(\phi)$ shows the rotation-modulated radio flux; the cyan vertical
line marks the current phase and the yellow dot is the current
intensity.

## Controls

Sliders set the magnetic obliquity $\alpha$, the observer inclination
$i$, the cone half-angle $\rho$ and the rotation period $P$. The
visibility classifier in the readout flags the geometric regime:
"single-pulse" (one cone crosses), "interpulse" (both cones cross,
near-orthogonal rotator like the Crab pulsar), or "missed" (no cone
crosses, no pulses).

Try $\alpha = 90^\circ$, $i = 88^\circ$: two equal pulses 180 deg
apart. Try $\alpha = 5^\circ$, $i = 70^\circ$: missed beam, no
pulses.
